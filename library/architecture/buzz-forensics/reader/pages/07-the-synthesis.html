The synthesis: where buzz sits, and what we take

Seven platforms ranked, three tiers of mechanism, and one recommendation

buzz Study — Batch 07: the synthesis#

Folder: library/architecture/buzz-forensics/ · Batch 07 of 07 · Written 2026-10-16 Source: library/architecture/platforms/_extracted/buzz-main/ — the extract of buzz-main.zip (md5 1219b15bef421e560e818fc000e7d72e), v0.5.28. Rules in force: 0b (count first) · 1 (docs are claims, code is authority) · 5 (no comparison to our own platform inside a batch — this batch keeps the same discipline and records cross-platform observations as observations, with the decisions left to the owner at the mapping pass) · 6 (name every capability) · 9 (pole-tag everything) · 10 (the retirement rule) · 13 (never let a number stand unverified).

What this batch is. Batches 01–06 read buzz. This batch does three things and nothing else:

  1. Ranks buzz's mechanisms — the ones that are evidence-backed and worth carrying, in the order I would act on them, each with the finding ids that back it.
  2. States what buzz contributes to the cross-platform mapping — the poles it is strong on, the poles it is absent on, and the one structural answer it gives that no earlier platform gave.
  3. Records the decided limits — the mechanisms that are good and that we will not adopt, with the reason, so a later agent does not re-litigate them.

It is not the mapping pass. It does not decide what we build. It hands the owner a ranked, sourced menu.

2. How buzz maps across the poles#

The workshop's method asks, for each pole, what the platform has. Measured, buzz's shape is sharp and unusual: it is the first platform in the study that is strong on two adjacent poles and a proof surface, and structurally absent on the pole the study keeps expecting.

Pole buzz's answer Strength
P1 · aissistant Real but thin, and deliberately so. Agent = keypair + channel membership; identity is a person's identity; memory is a custom NIP (NIP-AE); 6 tools, not 60; two binaries (buzz-agent, buzz-dev-mcp); the harness (buzz-acp) swaps either half. The default permission mode is bypass-permissions (F2-2). Medium — a genuine, minimal, standards-native P1; the safety default is the finding
P2 · workspace Strong. The workspace is the event log; one table, three lenses (Stream/Forum/Workflow differ by kind, not storage); 57 tables with a lintable tenant fence; membership-as-access with five role names. Strong
P3 · automations Real and event-native, ahead on security, behind on completeness. YAML definitions stored as canonical JSON; five triggers, seven actions (two are stubs); the approval gate is dead code; the cron is deterministic rather than locked. Medium
PX-W · widgets/composition Absent as a model — the fifth platform in the study with none. The composition unit is the event kind (136 registered in ALL_KINDS). The one composition-shaped surface (Channel Canvas, kind:40100) is a document with optimistic concurrency, not a widget tree. Absent
PX-F · frontend Desktop-first and polyglot. Desktop ~93% of the frontend; a 194k-line Rust bridge with 385 commands; mobile is a zero-Rust Dart re-implementation; web/admin ~1.1% combined. Strong
PZ · out-of-pole The strongest in the study, and the reason this platform was worth studying. Five safety artefacts across three languages — prose, TLA+, Tamarin. Very strong

The shape in one sentence. buzz is a communication substrate with a proof surface: it invests heavily in isolation, safety and the correctness of one shared log, and lightly in the things that sit on top of it — which is the exact inverse of a workflow tool.

3. buzz's one structural answer#

Every platform in this study has posed the same question in a different way: what is the unit you compose? The answers so far have mostly been UI or task units — a widget, a node, a bead, a step. buzz's answer is different, and it is the most checkable one:

The unit you compose is the event kind.

One kind: u32 registry (136 registered kinds), one append-only log, and every surface — a message, a reaction, a workflow step, a canvas edit, a code-review approval, a git event, an agent job — is the same object with a different integer. Extension means "add a kind", and the protocol (NIP-01) is public and shared, not bespoke (F1-1, F1-7, F5-2).

Why this is worth recording, whether or not we adopt it. It gives the study a clean counter-position to a composition model, and buzz states the trade honestly: an event log buys extensibility of data and behaviour (new kinds, new handlers, realtime fan-out, scoping, auth — for free); a widget model buys extensibility of layout and interface (someone who is not a programmer assembling a surface out of parts). buzz has the first in full and the second not at all, and it is coherent about it (F5 §7). The batch-05 question — "is an event log the same thing as a widget model?" — has a proven answer: No, and buzz is the clean demonstration of why.

The second structural answer, from PZ. buzz does not assert its isolation; it proves it and then checks the running code against the proof — a TLA+ model for isolation, a Tamarin model for authorization, a prose contract binding both to the deployment and to named code seams, and a runtime conformance crate that replays real executions against the model. No earlier platform in the study separates "the model" from "the running code" and then closes the gap with a gate. That separation is the transferable idea; the TLA+ toolchain is not required to copy it (F3-3, F6-12, F6-26).

4. Decided limits — good mechanisms we will not adopt, with the reason#

Recording these so a later agent does not re-open them. Each is strong in buzz and wrong for us now — not weak.

# Mechanism Why it is a decided limit
L-1 The event-log-as-everything architecture It is buzz's whole identity, and adopting it would replace our data model, our tenancy model and our UI model at once. Study the trade (extensibility of behaviour vs of layout); do not copy the substrate.
L-2 TLA+ / Tamarin as a toolchain The practice is transferable at one small scale (see #11); the toolchain is not. We do not have the invariants pinned well enough to mechanize, and adopting the tools before the invariants would invert the order rule 0 sets.
L-3 Nostr as the wire protocol Interesting as a proof that a public protocol can be the whole API; it is a re-platforming, not a mechanism to lift. The transferable part is "the wire format is the contract", recorded as an observation.
L-4 The polyglot frontend (Rust bridge + React + Dart + Flutter) buzz pays for it deliberately and can afford it; it multiplies our maintenance surface. The transferable parts are the guards and the token model, not the stack.
L-5 The 34-crate workspace The crate count is a consequence of the substrate choice, not a goal. Take the fences (lint, type, conformance); leave the topology.
L-6 bypass-permissions as a default It is a documented choice in buzz and it is the exact opposite of our default-deny posture (F2-2). Record it as a contrast, not a candidate.
L-7 The four formal models shipping in a product repo The practice (models as artefacts) is tier-2; shipping .tla/.spthy files is a decided limit for us until the invariants exist.

5. The honest ledger — what buzz does *not* have#

A synthesis that lists only strengths is marketing. Every item below is stated by buzz in-tree or measured in this study, and each is carried into the mapping pass as a known-open, not a surprise.

Structural absences (measured):

  • No widget/composer model — the fifth platform in the study with none (F5-1, F5-2).
  • No Postgres RLS — zero CREATE POLICY across 62 migrations; tenancy is application + lint enforced (F6-§5.3).
  • No peer-to-peer event path — the mesh transport (buzz-relay-mesh, iroh) is a separate question from the event path (F1-4).

Feature incompleteness (buzz's own, or measured in code):

  • Approval gates do not work — the whole grant/deny/resume path is dead code by measurement; the data model is finished, the mint is missing (§P3).
  • 2 of 7 automation actions are stubs (send_dm, set_channel_topic) — a definition using them saves and fails at run time (§P3).
  • The bypass-permissions default and a documented policy switch that does not exist (F2-2).
  • Cross-pod leaks are explicitly out of the conformance gate's scope, and the read-seam half of the gate is not armed (§PZ, LIMITS.md).

Aged artefacts (drift, not gaps):

  • PZ's own prose contract has aged behind the code on its P3/HA status — the shipped Redis replay guard and the chart's template-fail have closed the gap the doc still describes as open (D6-1). The code is stricter than the doc claims.
  • The rename hazard is a live metadata inconsistency — complete in code, incomplete in Cargo.toml's repository and AGENTS.md's topology table (F6-22, D6-3).

6. Rule 10 — the retirement surface#

buzz has the richest retirement surface in the study, and it is a real teaching case for the retirement rule.

  • 23 of 62 migrations carry a drop/remove/retire flavour.
  • The sharpest single retirement is the whole Phase-A NIP-FI authority ledger — migration 0044 drops 14 tables with CASCADE, with the reason recorded in the file itself (F1-9).
  • A product rename executed as scoped PRs (sprout → buzz; #958, #1012, #1043, #1333) with residue that never got renamed — the sprout repo URL, squareup/sprout-oss in AGENTS.md, and a crate directory still named sprig (F6-22). This is the second-order hazard: a rename done in code and left in metadata.
  • A hash encoding retired correctly — v1 retained, its ambiguity labelled and proven by a test, v2 shipped without invalidating history (F6-14, F6-15). This is rule 10 done right.

The lesson worth carrying: a rename is not finished when the code compiles; it is finished when the metadata — manifests, topology docs, CI names — has been grepped too, and when any residue is either renamed or documented as deliberate.

7. What this study did NOT do (limits of the evidence)#

Stated because a reader must know what is not behind these claims.

  • No .git — the extract has no history, so "the latest buzz" means this extract, at v0.5.28. The provenance is unusually rich (commit SHAs in the changelog) but the working tree is a snapshot (batch 00).
  • No execution — nothing here is a claim about runtime behaviour under load. Every claim is read from source (rule 1).
  • Unread surfaces, recorded as unread: the two crate-local denial modules (nip_fi/deny_map.rs, denial.rs) were not read (F6-§5.2); the Kubernetes provider's end-to-end state is unresolved (F2-7); the Flutter mobile tree was measured but not fully read (F5-§3.4).
  • The mobile client's parity is not verified — it is a zero-Rust re-implementation, and whether it agrees with the desktop on every kind is a contract with no lockstep check (D5-3).
  • No comparison to our own platform inside any batch (rule 5). Cross-platform observations live in this batch only, as observations; the decisions are the owner's at the mapping pass.

9. Ranked actions for the mapping pass#

The five actions I would put in front of the owner first, each sourced, each small, none requiring buzz's stack.

# Action Sourced from Cost
1 Adopt LIMITS.md for every gate, ratchet and guard we ship — name the blind spots, the owners, and any unarmed half. F6-12, F6-26 a file + a discipline
2 Adopt "prove the tripwire fires" — one mutation-style test per check, asserting the check catches the injected violation. F6-7, F6-12, F6-17 low
3 Adopt the guard-as-document pattern for our two or three most-broken frontend/architecture rules: a -core script, a per-app wrapper, a reason-annotated allowlist, a place in the default gate. F5-3, F5-4 low
4 Write the prose contract beside any formalism we produce — non-goals, one axiom per assumption, a gate per axiom, and the obligations that fail silently named by name. F6-26, D6-1 low
5 Put the rename hazard on the retirement checklist — grep the metadata (manifests, topology docs, CI names), not only the code, and document any deliberate residue. F6-22, D6-3 a checklist line

Recommendation. Take all five. They are the cheapest, most general mechanisms in the entire seven-platform study; four of the five are documents or tests, not systems; and each one has a stated wrong answer in the buzz source that tells us exactly what we would get wrong if we improvised it. The tenancy-tier mechanisms (Tier 2) are held for when the tenancy layer itself is built, where buzz is the study's best worked example — that is a mapping-pass decision, not a synthesis decision.

10. The verdict, in plain English#

Named. The buzz study is complete: seven batches, six findings documents plus the pin, every load-bearing claim checked against v0.5.28.

Plain meaning. buzz is a team workspace built on a public protocol, where every action — a message, a reaction, a workflow step, a code review, a git event — is a signed event with an integer type in one append-only log, and where the workspace's tenant isolation is not asserted but proven with formal methods and then re-checked against the running code. It invests heavily in isolation and correctness and lightly in the things above them.

Why it was worth the seventh slot. Because it closed the widget question with evidence (an event log is not a widget model, and buzz is the clean proof), because it is the only platform with a proof surface, and because it produced two mechanism families the study did not previously have: the guard-as-document pattern, and the gate-that-documents-its-own- blind-spots (LIMITS.md).

Risk. The study's claims are bounded by a snapshot with no git history and no execution; and buzz's own prose contract has aged behind its code on one safety status — which is itself the most instructive finding in the batch, because it is the second-order version of the rename hazard: a safety status left behind.

Ranked actions. Five, all cheap, all sourced, all in §9. Recommendation: take all five.

And the one thing to do before anything else: the buzz reader on port 3600, built from this markdown, using the shared engine (generate-reader.js + DOCS_CONFIG + diagrams.js) copied and rebranded — copy, never rewrite — then fidelity-verified the two ways the n8n reader taught us.

Batch 07 of 07 — the buzz study is written. Next: the buzz reader (port 3600), built from the markdown. Then, and only then, the ask: which platform is studied eighth — openrig, mattermost, or zulip (order not fixed; the owner decides).
